\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Prompt extraction and manipulation attacks against LLM applications have received considerable attention. PLeak~\cite{hui2024pleak} casts prompt extraction as an optimization problem and reconstructs system prompts almost completely from crafted queries. Multi-turn leakage attacks~\cite{multiturn2024} exploit the sycophancy of instruction-tuned models to recover hidden instructions incrementally, with success rates that reach 86.2\%. Systematic reviews of prompt injection~\cite{promptinjection2026} organize direct, indirect, and composed vectors and show that keeping the prompt confidential is not sufficient to guarantee secure behavior.

Software-based defenses respond to these threats at specific points of the interaction pipeline. ProxyPrompt~\cite{proxyprompt2025} interposes a proxy that shields the real prompt from extraction, and SysVec~\cite{sysvec2025} replaces the textual prompt with a non-invertible vector representation. Other proposals concentrate on validation and filtering: PromptGuard~\cite{promptguard2026} applies structured filtering against injection, Encrypted Prompt~\cite{encryptedprompt2025} gates prompt access through permission-bearing tokens, and PromptKeeper~\cite{promptkeeper2024} frames leakage detection as hypothesis testing. Attention Tracker~\cite{attentiontracker2025} inspects model internals to recognize attention patterns typical of adversarial inputs. These mechanisms are effective within their scope, but each operates inside a single trust boundary and targets one attack vector at a time.

A second line of work composes several defenses to improve robustness. TRYLOCK~\cite{trylock2026} stacks jailbreak defenses to lower attack success rates, and Countermind~\cite{countermind2025} adds auditing and monitoring capabilities to the inference pipeline. Analyses of serverless machine learning security~\cite{sas2026} describe the enlarged attack surface of cloud-native deployments. These contributions support the value of layered protection, yet they concentrate on inference-time robustness and leave cross-domain deployment unaddressed.

At the infrastructure level, TEE-based systems such as Petridish~\cite{petridish2024} and Omega~\cite{omega2025} run inference inside secure enclaves so that neither inputs nor prompts are directly accessible. Recent measurements report moderate overheads under controlled conditions~\cite{teeperf2025}. Nevertheless, these systems depend on specific hardware, constrain deployment options, and offer no built-in support for forensic attribution or cross-system monitoring.

Watermarking addresses the complementary problem of output provenance. Token-level schemes~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} were followed by scalable, production-oriented frameworks such as Waterfall~\cite{waterfall2024} and SynthID~\cite{synthid2024}. Zero-width character (ZWC) steganography~\cite{zheng2020zwc} embeds covert signals in generated text, although work on steganographic collusion~\cite{stegocollusion2024} shows that covert channels can also be abused. All of these techniques target output integrity and attribution, not the confidentiality of system prompts.

Taken together, the literature treats prompt confidentiality, input validation, output filtering, and attribution as separate problems, usually inside one trust boundary. The separation between deployment and ownership that characterizes multi-tenant settings adds constraints that these isolated solutions do not consider. A further gap concerns evidence: proposals that combine several mechanisms rarely report how much each component contributes, or whether the reported effectiveness holds when the underlying model changes. This article approaches prompt protection as a cross-domain trust problem, composes cryptographic protection, validation, filtering, watermarking, and monitoring across layers and administrative boundaries, and evaluates the resulting architecture across model families and layer configurations.
