From a systems perspective, the proposed trust model exchanges hardware-backed guarantees for broader deployability. Its root of trust is a correctly configured cloud IAM, which is weaker than hardware attestation but available on every provider. The defense-in-depth design mitigates this weakness: when one layer is compromised, for example through an IAM misconfiguration, the remaining layers continue to enforce protection. In this sense the architecture and TEE-based approaches are complementary rather than competing. Recent measurements show that TEE overhead has fallen considerably, below 10\% on CPUs and below 7\% on GPUs~\cite{teeperf2025}, so high-assurance deployments may combine both approaches.

The architecture generalizes across cloud providers, since every component maps to an equivalent service on Azure and GCP (Table~\ref{tab:mapping}), and portability does not alter the design principles. Several limitations remain. Rotating the HMAC signing key currently requires rebuilding the container image. Risk-scoring thresholds may need tuning per deployment. Text-processing pipelines that normalize Unicode may strip the zero-width watermark. The architecture has not been evaluated against adversarial fine-tuning, in which a model is retrained on captured prompt--response pairs, and cold-start latency in serverless environments may require provisioned concurrency. The adversarial evaluation is also static: runtime memory scraping by a privileged customer administrator ($\mathcal{A}_1$), who could read the decrypted prompt from process memory, was not tested, and neither were adaptive adversaries that refine their inputs from the observed blocking behavior. Per-account watermarked prompt variants make such leaks attributable rather than preventable, and adaptive red-teaming remains future work. Findings on steganographic collusion~\cite{stegocollusion2024} further indicate that covert signaling mechanisms carry risks of their own, which motivates additional monitoring.

From an ethical standpoint, the watermarking mechanism identifies accounts and sessions rather than individual users. Its use should be disclosed transparently and confined to intellectual property protection and forensic analysis, in line with standard privacy practices.
